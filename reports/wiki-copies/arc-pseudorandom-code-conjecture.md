# The pseudorandom-code conjecture

**True now (checked 2026-11-02; `inferred`: the opus-theory assistant's explanations to Kelvin, with a co-prover construction it had not checked itself; nothing was re-run).** Kelvin's informal picture, made precise by the assistant: take a late layer l with code c (an on/off pattern or a compressed state) and write the output mean as E[f] = Σ_c p(c)·m(c), with m(c) = E[f | c]. If the input-to-code map is "pseudorandom" and the code-conditional means m(c) vary, no efficient algorithm computes the sum to precision ε much faster than sampling, which costs about 1/ε². It is a conjecture, not a theorem. A worst-case witness exists (a built net, not a random one). Whether random He-initialised nets behave this way is open. The experiment Kelvin called weak, moment matching, finished (checked 2026-11-03): the input's low-order statistics alone leave the output mean badly undetermined (no tested net under 0.1 of the output's standard deviation at any degree run), so input-only methods are ruled out, as the conjecture's input-end picture predicts, and it says nothing about methods that track hidden layers. Whether he wants to engage ARC staff is not recorded; see "What Kelvin wants from ARC's plan". The route most likely to refute it, a chain over per-layer patterns, finished negative at widths 4–8 (checked 2026-11-03): gate patterns are few and structured, but no pattern-indexed summary tried, including a Gaussian per pattern, a mixture and a Gaussian per path with about 20x more states than cells, gets within 100x of sampling, so the hoped-for factorization is absent for those summaries (the deep-net plateau in cell counts turned out to be the net dying, not structure). The layer-wise cumulant lane's report (checked 2026-11-03) is in: the oracle form of the bound is false (exact order-d statistics of every hidden layer nearly pin the mean), the worst-case form is true but 14–30x larger than ARC's actual error, and the typical case is the open crux: ARC's ladder at depth n/8 is the per-step closure error, about c/n, simply added up, and each extra order buys a fixed factor while costing about n times more, so a convincing negative must be a lower bound on typical per-step closure error (see "Queued experiment and the stronger targets", target 1). The conjecture is a claim about hardness; the bit counts on [[bit-accounting-lane-method]] only locate where the difficulty sits and do not bound it.

## The statement and its catch
- **Pseudorandom codes alone are not enough.** If m(c) depended on the code additively, unit by unit, the sum would split into easy pieces. The hard case needs the mean to depend on the code jointly, through high-order structure. The working form: the output mean depends on the input law only through high-order correlations (roughly growing with depth) that behave pseudorandomly.
- **What refutes it** is ARC's hope: a short "explanation" of the high-order structure of random nets that an algorithm can exploit. The conjecture says random deep ReLU nets have no explanation beyond low-order structure.

## Evidence for it (the lanes' numbers, relayed)
- Exact linear regions grow 3–4x per unit of width, and integrating them exactly beats sampling only to widths 6–8 ([[linearization-graph-and-linear-experts]] has the region counts and the cone tree).
- The output's dependence on the input has mean Hermite degree about 21 at 256 x 32, spread over about n/4 directions, and the spread grows with width.
- Every method that summarises low-order structure loses at depth: ARC's moment ladder, closures and learned experts ([[arc-matching-sampling-bar]] describes the moment-propagation methods).

## The order-127 witness (worst case only)
From the randomness lane's co-prover, proven in `work/randomness/coprover/astra_r1_answer.md` by its account; the assistant had not checked the proof.
- **Input law.** 256 Gaussian coordinates; of the first 128, keep the sizes |xᵢ| independent but force the product of their signs to be +1 (a parity constraint). Any 127 or fewer coordinates still look exactly like independent Gaussians, so every statistic on at most 127 coordinates matches the real input.
- **Net.** f(x) = ReLU(product of the signs of x₁…x₁₂₈ × the smallest |xᵢ| among them). It is bias-free and fits a width-256 ReLU net, using (|a+b| − |a−b|)/2 = sign(ab)·min(|a|,|b|) in a binary tree of 7 levels.
- **Gap.** Under real Gaussian inputs the parity is +1 half the time, so E[f] = ½·E[min |Gᵢ|]. Under the constrained law it is always +1, so E[f] = E[min |Gᵢ|]: a factor of 2.
- **Reach.** A ReLU net can compute a high-order parity, after which statistics up to order 127 say nothing about its mean. The co-prover itself calls it "a worst-case counterexample within the architecture, not a lower bound for iid Gaussian weights". The assistant first described it more strongly than this and corrected itself when Kelvin asked.

## Is "pseudorandom against degree-d tests" probably true?
The assistant's answer: partly. Precisely, the property means input statistics up to degree d do not pin down the output mean; equivalently, no degree-d polynomials p₋ ≤ f ≤ p₊ have close Gaussian means.
- In the literal sense, no: about 22% of the output variance is linear in the input, the net is Lipschitz and stable at critical initialisation (unlike a hash), and crude precision is set by low-order statistics, which is why ARC's methods do well at rough accuracy.
- A graded version is defensible: the degree needed for precision ε grows fast as ε shrinks. A heuristic noise-stability fit puts the weight above degree k falling only like k^(−2/3); if that holds, the degree needed for precision ε grows like a power of 1/ε, far faster than sampling's 1/ε² samples. That is the conjecture in measurable form.
- Evidence to gather, strongest first: (1) moment-matching bounds, the largest and smallest E[f] over all input laws that match the Gaussian's moments up to degree d (a linear program over many sample points, feasible for widths 4–8 and degrees up to about 4–8; measured, see "Queued experiment and the stronger targets"); (2) the Hermite spectrum of f, whose heavy tail is necessary but not sufficient; (3) how well the best degree-d polynomial predicts the code or output.

## Candidate structures that could refute it (checked 2026-11-03; `inferred`)
After the cone tree finished (merging fails: the number of merged groups grows as fast as the regions, see [[linearization-graph-and-linear-experts]]), Kelvin asked for "any possible higher-order structure like ARC would dream of". Merging fails because the number of distinct regions grows exponentially; ARC's hope is that the sum over regions factorizes, so it can be computed without listing them. The assistant gave three forms:
1. **Factorizing across layers (the closest to Kelvin's internal-means picture; the simple chain and richer per-pattern states were tested and failed, see the results below).** Regions are combinations of per-layer patterns, and per-layer patterns are few even where regions are many: on the 4×4 net, layer by layer there are 16, 5, 5 and 6 distinct patterns but 103–200 joint regions. If the pattern sequence behaved like a chain (each layer depending mainly on the one before), the mean would follow from a dynamic program over layers whose state is one internal mean per per-layer pattern, costing like the per-layer pattern count and not the joint count. The error is how far the sequence is from a chain, measurable exactly at widths 4–6.
2. **Independence within a layer (untested).** If groups of units in a layer switched nearly independently given the layer below, regions would be a product of small factors, like a tensor network with low bond dimension. The test is the mutual information between groups of gates and how it grows with width; the bit counts hint it is large near the input but were not measured this way.
3. **Averaging over many units (already priced).** Sums over many units concentrate, so pre-activations look Gaussian: ARC's moment and cumulant view. Each extra order buys less as width grows and it loses to sampling at depth n/8.

The assistant's read when it was proposed: number 1 was the only one not yet tested. On widths 4–6 one can compute exactly how much a layer chain loses against the exact region sum and how that loss grows with width. If the loss stays small while regions explode, that is the higher-order structure ARC would want; if it grows like the regions, it is strong evidence for the conjecture. Kelvin approved it at 02:28Z on 3 November (see "What Kelvin wants from ARC's plan"), after confirming that "patterns" means gate patterns: which units of a layer are on and off for an input.

The approved task maps every on/off pattern at depth 4, widths 4 to 8, three nets each, widths 4–6 reporting first. It tracks five things as width grows: (a) the chain test, a dynamic program over per-layer patterns compared with the exact region sum and with both kinds of sampling; (b) how many earlier bits, or which few units, fix each later layer's pattern; (c) sparse or low-rank transitions between consecutive layers' patterns; (d) independent groups of units, so regions split into small blocks; (e) units that do not affect the output mean. Kelvin then allowed depths 2 to 16 at small width and larger widths with sampled pattern statistics, on one GPU capped at $8. If any of (a)–(e) holds while the region count explodes, the problem is much simpler.

**Results, depth 4, widths 4–8 (relayed; the lane's outside review reproduced the trends).** (b), (c) and (e) hold in a weak sense and (a) fails. Only 1.5–4% (width 4) down to 0.04–0.2% (width 8) of pattern combinations occur, each later unit depends on about 2.5–6 earlier bits, transitions are sparse and low-rank in effect (99%-energy rank about 3n, exact rank much higher), and 1–7 of 16–24 units are irrelevant to the mean. But the chain with one mean per pattern errs by 4×10⁻³ to 0.22 against sampling's 9.5×10⁻⁷, the full pattern history leaves 5×10⁻⁴ to 0.09, and richer states do not rescue it (a Gaussian per pattern 10⁻³ to 2×10⁻², a mixture slowly better, a Gaussian per path 5×10⁻⁴ to 5×10⁻³ with about 20x more states than cells, splitting on the 2–5 parent bits no better than the full history). The assistant's reason, which it and the review call heuristic: inside a cell the state is a truncated linear image of a Gaussian, and the next gate depends on where it sits in its cone, so a per-pattern summary loses it and exact bookkeeping brings back one state per cell. The apparent plateau in cell counts at depth is the net dying (all states collapse to one direction, then every unit is dead; the known dying of deep narrow ReLU nets), so it is no structure. The negative is limited to the summary types tested; it supports the conjecture only for this family. Details on [[linearization-graph-and-linear-experts]], "Gate-pattern mapping".

## What would make it a theorem
1. A restricted lower bound: any method using only degree-≤d statistics of the input (or of each layer) has error at least X. The order-127 example is the witness shape; this is the most promising formal version.
2. A conditional bound: if the map to the code is pseudorandom against degree-d tests, then a lower bound holds. Proving random nets satisfy the premise is the open part.
3. An unconditional bound for all algorithms would be a super-linear computational lower bound, out of reach.

## Queued experiment and the stronger targets
Kelvin said to queue the moment-matching experiment, "but that still seems quite weak". The assistant agreed: it rules out only methods using nothing but the input's low-order statistics, while ARC's methods use the weights and their moments are those of hidden layers. It was queued on CPU at widths 4, 6, 8 and depths 1, 2, 4, 8, with a linear-net control and a scaled-down parity example. It finished on 3 November (checked 2026-11-03; `inferred`, MEASURED and exploratory, relayed from the lane's final message and the assistant's summary; the lane could not write its own `report.md` because the harness blocks subagent report files, so the assistant filed `outbox/moment-matching-2026-11-03.md`; files in `work/moment-matching/` on General: `cg.py`, `runs/`).
  - **Result.** The undetermined part of the output mean, in standard deviations of the output (median over 3 nets; values at the highest degrees are lower bounds because those runs stopped early): at width 4, 1.1–1.8 at degree 2, 0.6–1.3 at degree 4, 0.4–0.9 at degree 6, 0.3–0.7 at degree 8; at width 8, 1.4–2.6 at degree 2 and 0.7–1.6 at degree 4 (ranges are over depths 1–8). No net got under 0.1 sd at any degree run (the lowest was 0.21 sd); odd degrees add almost nothing. A single 1-D ReLU needs degree 16 to reach 0.1 sd.
  - **Extrapolation (a fit, not a measurement).** The gap falls roughly like 1/degree, so 0.1 sd needs degree about 20–60 and 0.01 sd degree about 200–650.
  - **Depth.** At width 4 depth raises the gap but not its decay rate (exponent 0.9–1.1). At widths 6–8 deeper nets have 1.5–2.5x larger gaps and shrink more slowly from degree 2 to 4, so the needed degree probably grows with depth at larger width; too few degrees were run there to fit a rate.
  - **Method and controls.** A fixed sample of 2¹⁶ points was still growing about 3% per doubling, so the gaps come from adding points continuously. The linear-net control gives exactly zero. The scaled-down parity control sits above its built-in bound of 1.26 sd but does not drop at degree d = n as expected; the assistant's summary mentions only the linear control, so treat the parity control as unexplained.
  - **Reading (the assistant's).** With the cumulant lane: the input's statistics alone pin down very little, while the true statistics of every hidden layer would pin the mean down almost completely, so the difficulty sits in getting internal layers' statistics right, the per-step closure crux of target 1. It fits Kelvin's scrambling picture at the input end, not for methods that track internal layers. It confirms his "quite weak" verdict on the test's reach: it rules out only input-only methods.
- **Stronger target 1 (within reach; running as its own lane, first result in).** A lower bound for ARC's own class: methods that carry each layer as its cumulants up to order d. The original question was whether two worlds exist whose layer-wise cumulants up to order d agree at every layer yet whose output means differ (the representation-ambiguity negative that gpt-6 proposed; the moment-matching test is its input-layer special case). The lane's first result shows that framing aims at the wrong target: if the true statistics up to order d of every hidden layer were known, the output mean would be nearly pinned, leaving a one-dimensional question about the last layer's pre-activation, so matching every layer leaves no room for parity-like ambiguity (a simple proof, not yet checked by the co-prover). ARC does not know those statistics; it propagates them forward, and a layer's order-d statistics do not determine the next layer's. The lane was reframed around that closure step: (1) per-step ambiguity, how much the next layer's order-d statistics can still vary given the true ones, which is the unavoidable error per step of any order-d method; (2) over all layers, starting from the exact input and allowing anything consistent at each step, how far the output mean can spread as depth grows, the worst case over all order-d closures; (3) whether ARC's actual errors sit near that worst case or leave room for a better closure. If (2) grows with depth even as d grows, that is the restricted negative in ARC's own terms.
  - **First co-prover round (relayed, 3 November; proofs not seen).** Proven: knowing every layer's statistics exactly reduces the problem to the last layer; after the first step the worst-case closure error has an exact formula, about 1.7/d of a ReLU unit's standard deviation, the same at every width; averaged over random weights that worst case grows with depth because each layer adds kurtosis. Measured on the tiny 4-wide net, at orders 2–3 the worst case over all order-d closures leaves the output mean undetermined by about 1 standard deviation, against ARC's real per-neuron error of 0.01–0.03, so the worst case is 10–40x too large. Reading: random nets' layer laws are not adversarial and ARC's closures exploit their typical structure, so the negative has to be about typical structure. The lane was steered to the typical case (best order-d closure error per step on true layer laws, its accumulation with depth, and whether improvement with d slows with width and depth).
  - **The lane's final report (`outbox/cumulant-ambiguity-2026-11-03.md`, bead pool-ttdzrk.19; relayed, `inferred`; its PROVEN labels rest on the co-prover gpt-6-astra, whose proofs this wiki has not seen).** Three forms of the conjecture, each with a verdict:
    1. **Oracle form: false.** If the true order-≤d moments of every hidden layer up to the last were known, the remaining ambiguity is one 1D ReLU moment problem at the last pre-activation. For a Gaussian pre-activation its spread is 0.86 of the ReLU's standard deviation at d = 2–3, 0.36 at d = 4–5 and about 1.69/d for large d, so it goes to zero with d. (The framing "agree at every layer up to the output yet means differ" is impossible as written, since the output mean is linear in the last activation layer's mean.) Parity-type constructions defeat input-only matching, not this oracle; there is no general lower bound of the form gap ≥ c·Gamma_d.
    2. **Worst-case closure form: true but vacuous for ARC.** One step can move a next-layer mean by that same 0.86 / 0.36 sd at any width; over depth at n = 4 the output mean can move by about 1 sd (finite-support linear programs, lower bounds). ARC's real error is 0.009–0.03 sd, 14–30x smaller (the earlier relay said 10–40x), so random nets' layer laws are far from adversarial. First-hidden-layer laws are identified by their moments, so the first step is exact.
    3. **Typical form: open, the crux.** Conjecture: no local order-d closure rule beats the typical per-step error c_K(l/n)/n by more than a constant. If true, every moment hierarchy's precision exponent goes to zero along depth proportional to width, which is the restricted negative ARC would have to answer. Measured support: ARC's closure has exactly this shape and max-entropy closure does not beat it; nothing proves the lower bound. Averaged over weights the last pre-activation's kurtosis is 3(1+2/n)(1+5/n)^(L-1), so order-4 information is worth less as L/n grows (proved for the weight average, heuristic for fixed weights).
  - **Where the typical-case work points (measured, heuristic mechanism).** The earlier lead (early-layer errors amplified downstream) is refuted for ARC's shape: at depth n/8 the recursion is 1.0–2.2x the plain sum of per-step errors. Numbers, the amplification regime at depth n/4 and beyond, and what remains unexplained are on [[arc-matching-sampling-bar]], "Random, untrained networks".
- **Stronger target 2 (harder).** A reduction from a believed-hard problem: beating sampling on random nets would solve it. Nobody knows how to do this for random rather than planted nets. Kelvin wants to think about which hard problems relate and deferred the discussion.
